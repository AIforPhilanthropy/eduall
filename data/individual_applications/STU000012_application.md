## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I am in the first year of my Master of Law at the University of Zurich, specialising in international economic law, and I am applying to EduAll to make sure that the last stage of my degree, my thesis, receives the time it requires.

My monthly costs amount to CHF 2,431. The largest item is rent: CHF 1,417 for a studio in Wiedikon, which I took after two previous shared flats ended abruptly. In Zurich, a student without a Swiss guarantor has very little choice. Tuition and semester fees add CHF 203 a month, and the remainder covers food, insurance and transport.

I earn CHF 935 a month as a student assistant at a legal-aid organisation for migrants, where I prepare case files and translate for Bengali- and Hindi-speaking clients. My father, a district court advocate in Sylhet, and my mother, a secondary school teacher, have a combined income of around CHF 700. They send me CHF 120 every month, which is about a sixth of what they earn, and they are also putting my younger brother through engineering school.

This leaves a monthly gap of CHF 1,376. Over the past semester I have filled it by taking on extra hours, but my thesis on the enforcement of labour standards in global garment supply chains requires sustained research, including interviews with NGOs in Geneva.

A grant from EduAll would allow me to hold my working hours at a sustainable level and to complete my degree in January 2028 with a thesis I am proud of.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

Last November, I sat in the public gallery of the Federal Supreme Court in Lausanne and watched five judges deliberate openly on a case involving a Bangladeshi textile supplier. I had read about Swiss public deliberations, but seeing it was something else: judges disagreeing respectfully, in public, on the reasoning behind a decision that would affect workers thousands of kilometres away. I took the train back to Zurich knowing exactly what I wanted my thesis to be about.

I grew up in Sylhet, in north-eastern Bangladesh. When I was sixteen, my father was arrested for representing a group of garment workers in a wage dispute and held for four months without charge. Our family's income stopped overnight. My mother kept us going on her teacher's salary, and I tutored younger students to pay for my own school fees. I watched my father's colleagues, and later the courts, eventually secure his release. That experience made me believe in law, and also showed me how fragile it can be.

I completed my LLB at the University of Dhaka and came to Zurich in February 2026. Studying law in a new legal system, partly in German, has been demanding, but I have completed 4 courses, and my work at the legal-aid organisation has taught me as much as any seminar.

I serve as president of the Bangladeshi Students' Association in Zurich, where I organised a series of information evenings on tenant rights and employment law for newly arrived students and workers, together with volunteer lawyers.

After graduating, I want to work on corporate accountability for supply chains, first in an international organisation in Geneva and eventually back in Bangladesh, where millions of workers depend on standards that exist mainly on paper. Education, for me, is the means to make those standards real.
