## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I want to tell you about my student life as it really is, because the numbers alone look cold.

Each month my costs are CHF 1,590. The biggest part is CHF 615 for my room, then food CHF 319, and fees CHF 203. The rest is health insurance, the tram, my phone, and books and a second-hand laptop I pay off slowly.

I study part-time and work about 19 hours a week at the IT help desk of an insurance company, and I earn CHF 2,132. My mother in Lahore still sends CHF 50 every month. I know what this means. Our family lives on about CHF 230. I have asked her to stop, and she refuses. I also receive a scholarship of CHF 3,095 a year from a Zurich student fund, which I am deeply grateful for.

The room I have now is a temporary one, through a housing association for young people, and it ends in January. I have lived in five places since I arrived. From September 2027 my second-year project courses need more time, and I will reduce my hours to about CHF 1,240; then CHF 300 a month will be missing.

I give many hours to my student association and to the community, and I would not want to give that up. With EduAll's support I could finally have a stable room and study with a calm heart until I graduate in January 2031.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

The first time I understood a lecture at the University of Zurich fully in German, it was a Tuesday in November 2025, in Algorithms and Data Structures. The professor made a joke, and I laughed at the same time as everyone else. I nearly cried in the lecture hall. For nine months, I had been laughing a few seconds late.

I grew up in Lahore, in a small flat above a tailor's shop. My mother raised me and my two younger siblings alone. She taught me that you do not waste anything, not food, not time, not a chance.

I came to Switzerland at nineteen, and I live here now under provisional admission: my asylum application was rejected, but the authorities recognised that I could not safely return to the people who had threatened our family. I started my studies in February 2025. German was, and still is, my biggest challenge. I record every lecture, and I read the slides twice, once for the words and once for the meaning.

Even so, I have found a home. I am the elected coordinator of the international students' group in the computer science student association, and I organise our study tandems, where German-speaking students help newcomers and newcomers help with programming. I also volunteer every Saturday at a coding club for refugee teenagers in Altstetten. When a fourteen-year-old shows me the first game they made, I remember why I love this field.

I want to become a software engineer and one day build tools that help people learn languages faster, so that nobody has to laugh a few seconds late for months.

Education, for me, is my mother's sacrifice turned into something that lasts.
