## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

Let me say who I am: a mother of two, a former pharmacist, and a second-year chemistry student at forty-three, studying part-time.

I work as a pharmacy assistant about 19 hours a week and earn CHF 2,152. My husband works as a mechanic in Basel and earns around CHF 4,700 a month. He covers CHF 390 of my share of our household. My own costs are CHF 1,935. On paper, our household manages.

But it manages because we have both stretched ourselves to the limit. I work in the pharmacy on evenings and Saturdays, study in the mornings, and care for our children, who are eight and eleven, in the afternoons. What is left of what we brought from Kharkiv is our only protection if something goes wrong. Next year my laboratory courses become much heavier, and I do not know how I will keep all my pharmacy hours.

I am asking for support so that my studies do not take everything our family has. My degree ends in January 2030.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

In Kharkiv I was a pharmacist for fifteen years. I knew every medicine, every dose, every interaction. In February 2022 the war came to our city. I kept the pharmacy open as long as I could, handing out insulin and heart pills to neighbours who could not leave.

We came to Switzerland in April 2022. My diploma was not recognised. I started again: German from zero, a job as a pharmacy assistant, evenings with vocabulary cards. For two years I volunteered as an interpreter for Ukrainian families at a Basel community centre, and I helped organise a Ukrainian Saturday school for children.

At forty-two I began a bachelor's in chemistry, so that one day I can have my own profession again, and perhaps move into pharmaceutical research.

My children do their homework next to me at the kitchen table. My daughter asks me to explain chemistry to her. My son asks me when I will be "a real pharmacist again". Soon, I tell him. Soon.
