## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

Every Tuesday evening for the past two years I have sat in a small room at a Geneva community centre with elderly Vietnamese women who never learned French. I help them read their letters from the health insurance, the commune, the doctor. Sometimes we just drink tea. They call me "con", my child. It is the best part of my week.

I am a doctoral student in Public Health at the University of Geneva, in my third year. My project, on access to health care for elderly migrants, was financed by a two-year fellowship that ended in August. My supervisor has confirmed that a new grant will cover my salary from 2028; until then I have a teaching assistant mandate of CHF 765 a month.

My parents, who run a small Vietnamese restaurant in Geneva and earn around CHF 5,000 a month together, give me CHF 1,424 for rent and living costs. My costs are CHF 1,980. My rent is CHF 1,156 for a studio near the university.

My parents have always given me everything. They work twelve hours a day, six days a week, and they never had the chance to study. I want to lighten their load in these months until my funding returns, and not take from them money they need for their own old age.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

My parents came to Geneva in 1998 with nothing, and opened their restaurant with money borrowed from friends. I grew up between the kitchen and the dining room, doing homework on the corner table. They never went to school beyond a few years. When I received my acceptance letter for university, and later for my doctorate, my mother framed both and hung them next to the cash register.

I studied public health because I saw, through our customers and neighbours, how many elderly migrants live alone, sick, and invisible to the health system. In my bachelor's I volunteered with the Red Cross, and during my master's I led a student initiative that trained young volunteers to accompany elderly migrants to medical appointments. We trained forty volunteers in two years.

My doctoral research documents the barriers that elderly migrants face in the Swiss health system, and tests a model of community health mediators.

I want to build a career in public health research and policy, so that the old women I meet on Tuesdays are no longer alone with their letters.
