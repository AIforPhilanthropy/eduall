## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I am entering the last four semesters of my Bachelor's in Educational Sciences at the University of Basel, and I am applying to EduAll to ensure that the most important part of my degree, the practical teaching placement and my thesis, can be completed without financial pressure dictating my choices.

My monthly expenses amount to CHF 1,828: CHF 757 for a room in a shared flat in Gundeldingen, CHF 285 for health insurance, CHF 142 for tuition and semester fees, and modest costs for food, transport and study materials. I earn CHF 752 a month as a classroom assistant in a day school in Basel, where I support children who are learning German as an additional language. My sister and her husband, both teachers in Peshawar with a combined income of around CHF 900 a month, send me CHF 240. They have three children of their own; this is a sacrifice I do not take lightly.

This leaves a gap of CHF 836 each month. My placement this autumn requires me to be in a partner school four days a week, which means reducing my hours at the day school substantially. Without additional support, the gap will widen exactly when I most need to concentrate on teaching well.

An EduAll grant would allow me to complete my placement and thesis on schedule, graduating in July 2028, and to begin working in education immediately afterwards.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

I grew up in Peshawar, in a family of teachers. My mother taught in a government primary school with classes of fifty children in rooms built for thirty, one blackboard and a box of chalk that had to last the term. As a girl I corrected her pupils' copybooks on the kitchen floor. I learned early that a child who is seen by a teacher, even for a moment each day, learns differently from a child who is not.

In December 2014, when I was fourteen, a school in our city was attacked. Among those killed were children from our street and a teacher my mother had trained with. For months afterwards, parents were afraid to send their children to any school, and teachers were afraid to go. My mother kept teaching. In the evenings I went with her to visit families at home and persuade them to let their children return. By the end of that school year, almost all of her pupils were back.

That period taught me that education is not only about curriculum. It is about trust, safety and the relationship between a school and its community. I became a teacher myself, taught for two years, and wanted to study these questions seriously. In 2024 I came to Basel, which has a strong tradition in comparative and inclusive education.

Studying in German, far from home, has been a humbling experience. But my work as a classroom assistant has given me daily contact with Swiss schools and with children from Afghanistan, Eritrea, Syria and Ukraine who are navigating a new language, as I am. My thesis examines how Swiss day schools build relationships with refugee families, and it draws on everything I learned in Peshawar.

After graduating, I want to work in teacher training focused on schools in crisis-affected regions, and eventually to return to Pakistan to support teachers working in insecure areas.

Education, to me, is an act of courage by children, parents and teachers alike. I want to spend my career supporting that courage.
