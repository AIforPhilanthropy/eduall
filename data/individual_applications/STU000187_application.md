## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

How long can a doctorate survive on savings? I have been asking myself this since March.

I am in my second year of a doctorate in Public Health at the University of Bern, studying access to mental health care for refugees. When I started, my supervisor told me that a project grant would pay my salary from the second year. In March the grant was rejected. My supervisor has written to confirm that no salaried position is available until a new application is decided in 2028. I now have only a small teaching assistant mandate, CHF 602 a month.

My sister in Erbil, a school headteacher, contributes CHF 600, although the whole household lives on roughly CHF 2,200.

My monthly costs are CHF 2,154. At my age health insurance alone is CHF 290, and CHF 89 goes into fieldwork materials and translation costs for interviews that my project needs. My rent is CHF 1,187 plus CHF 103 utilities.

I have lived in Switzerland for sixteen years and have worked for all of them. I did not expect, at forty-three, to find myself counting every franc again. But I have come too far in this research to stop. With EduAll's support I could continue until my funding is restored.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

What happens to a mind that has seen too much? I have asked this question since I was a child in Baghdad, during the war of 1991, when my uncle came home from the front and never spoke again about what he had seen.

My parents were both teachers. I studied medicine in Baghdad, but the violence after 2003 made practising impossible for me. In 2010 I came to Switzerland. My diploma was only partly recognised, so for many years I worked as an interpreter in hospitals and asylum centres, and later as a research assistant in a public health institute.

As an interpreter, I have sat with hundreds of refugees telling their stories to doctors. I have seen how often their suffering goes untreated, because no one understands them, or because the system does not see them.

I completed a master's in public health in Bern and began my doctorate in 2024. I have published five articles on refugee mental health. My research compares access to mental health services for refugees in three Swiss cantons.

I want to make mental health care reachable for people who have survived what my uncle survived, and who deserve better than silence.
