## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I am in the second year of my Master of Law at the University of St. Gallen, which I expect to complete in July 2028.

My monthly expenses are CHF 2,455. The largest items are rent (CHF 1,025 for a small flat I share with my two children), health insurance (CHF 220) and food (CHF 390), followed by fees of CHF 522, utilities, transport, phone and study materials. My sibling in Pune, whose household lives on about CHF 1,840, sends me CHF 460 a month. I have no earned income. My monthly shortfall is CHF 1,995.

I want to be fully transparent about my savings of CHF 10,452. This is the remainder of a settlement I received after my divorce in 2023, and it has been covering the monthly gap since then. At the current rate, it will last roughly six more months. I have deliberately kept a reserve to cover the first months after graduation, while I sit the bar-equivalent examinations and look for a traineeship, because my children depend entirely on me.

I have not been able to take paid work during my master's. My children are eleven and fourteen, and as a single parent I am responsible for them every afternoon and evening. My final semester involves a thesis and a moot court competition, which will take all remaining hours.

An EduAll grant would allow me to finish my degree without spending the reserve that must carry our family through the transition into work.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

I am forty-five, a parent of two, and a student of law, in that order of seniority and not always in that order of priority.

I grew up in a village in Maharashtra, where my father was a farm labourer and my mother a domestic worker. I was the first in my family to finish school, and in my late twenties I completed a law degree in Pune by distance learning. I never practised. Instead, I found my way into law through my community: I led a women's self-help federation of more than forty groups, which negotiated microcredit terms with banks and helped members claim land titles in their own names. There, I learned that the law is only as strong as the people who know how to use it.

In 2019 my family moved to Switzerland for my then-spouse's work. After our separation, I decided to turn my experience into formal qualifications, and I was admitted to the master's programme in St. Gallen in 2024. The first year was difficult, balancing new legal traditions, a new language and two children adjusting to a new school. My results are solid rather than outstanding, but I have completed five courses and have never missed an exam.

I also co-ordinate a legal information circle for migrant women in St. Gallen, where we explain rights around residence, divorce and custody.

I intend to specialise in family and migration law and to practise with a legal aid organisation. For me, education is the difference between knowing you have rights and being able to use them.
