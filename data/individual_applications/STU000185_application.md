## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

My mother always told me: "Never hide your hunger from people who can feed you." So I will not hide anything.

I am in my first year of Chemistry at the University of Zurich, which I study part-time. My costs are CHF 2,085 per month. I receive a scholarship of CHF 7,782 per year from a Swiss refugee education foundation, which is a great help. I work about 13 hours a week in the chemistry department, washing and preparing laboratory glassware, and earn CHF 1,423. It is work I can do well with one good eye, because it needs care, not small print. My friend and former colleague in Accra, a lab manager, still sends me CHF 210 every month out of a household income of about CHF 880; he says it is his way of fighting what happened to me.

Even with all this, every month there is a gap of CHF 452. My room costs CHF 722, the cheapest I found. I cook for three days at a time. I do not complain; I am free, and I am studying.

A grant from EduAll would close the gap and let me focus on the chemistry, which is the reason I came.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

My mother was a market woman in Kumasi who sold cloth, and she could read people better than any book. When I was fifteen, she looked at me and said, "Whatever you are, you are my child." I did not understand then how much courage that sentence would need.

I studied chemistry at KNUST and worked for twelve years as a laboratory analyst for a mining company, testing water and soil for cyanide and mercury. I was the head of a team of six analysts. I was good at my work, and I was careful to be invisible outside it.

In 2023, after Ghana's parliament moved to criminalise LGBTQ people, my name was shared in a group of men who wanted to "clean" our town. I was attacked outside my house. I lost most of the sight in my left eye. I left Ghana two months later and was recognised as a refugee in Switzerland.

In Zurich I started again at thirty-eight, in a new language, as a first-year student. It is hard; German chemistry lectures move fast. But I volunteer every week at a support group for LGBTQ refugees, and I see that I am not alone.

My mother died last year. She did not see me start university again. I will finish for her, and one day I want to work on clean water for mining communities.
