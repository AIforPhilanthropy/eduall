## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I began my Bachelor of Law at the University of St. Gallen in February 2026, after three years of full-time work, and I am determined to complete it. To do so, I need a stable financial basis for the next two and a half years.

My monthly expenses are CHF 1,847. Tuition and semester fees at HSG are higher than at most Swiss universities and come to CHF 205 a month. My rent is CHF 911 for a furnished room that I found at short notice when I moved to St. Gallen; it is on a month-to-month basis, and the landlord has already said he may need it back in the spring.

My mother, who works part-time in a care home in Wil, earns around CHF 2,100 a month and gives me CHF 750 of it. It is far more than she can comfortably spare, and she does it because she never had the opportunity to study herself. I also receive a small scholarship of CHF 1,559 a year from my home municipality.

The difference between my expenses and my monthly support is CHF 1,097. HSG's first-year assessment is known for its intensity, and I have deliberately not taken a job during it, so that I can pass. From my second year, I plan to work part-time again.

An EduAll grant would give me the security to complete the assessment year, find a stable room, and continue towards my degree, expected in January 2029.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

I am twenty-three, I grew up between Kumasi and Wil, and I am the first person in my family to study at a university.

I came to Switzerland at eleven, when my mother received her residence permit after years of working here while I stayed with my grandmother in Ghana. I arrived speaking English and Twi, and I was placed in a class where I understood nothing. Swiss German was a wall. But I learned it, and when I finished secondary school, I did what was expected of a child in my position: a commercial apprenticeship.

I spent three years as an apprentice and then three more as a commercial employee in the legal department of an insurance company in St. Gallen. I prepared files, checked contracts and took minutes in meetings with lawyers. I noticed that I understood the cases better than I was supposed to. One of the lawyers, a woman who had also come to law late, encouraged me to take the university entrance exam. I passed it on my first attempt while working full-time.

At HSG, I am often the only person in the room whose parents did not go to university and who has already worked for a living. I see that as a strength. I know how organisations actually function, and I know what it means for a contract to affect a real person's income.

I want to specialise in labour and social insurance law, and eventually to work for an organisation that helps people, especially migrants, navigate the social insurance system, which I watched my mother struggle with for years.

Education, for me, is the chance my mother worked so hard to give me. I intend to use it well.
