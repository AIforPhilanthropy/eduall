## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

For three years, every weekend and many evenings, I have worked as a nursing aide in a care home in Bern. I wash, feed and turn residents who can no longer do these things themselves. I earn CHF 1,079 a month, and together with CHF 130 that my parents send me from Accra, this has been exactly enough. My father is a pharmacist and my mother a midwife; their household income is around CHF 810, and they are also supporting my two younger sisters at university in Ghana.

But from January, my medical master's enters the clinical rotations. Each rotation lasts several weeks, full days in the hospital, with night and weekend duties. My employer has been kind, but a care home cannot schedule around a medical student's rotation plan. I will lose most of my shifts, and with them most of my income.

My monthly costs are CHF 1,648: a room in a shared flat in Länggasse for CHF 652, health insurance of CHF 235, tuition and the rest. I have kept them as low as I can.

What I am asking for is simple: the chance to spend my clinical years learning to be a doctor, and not calculating how many night shifts I need to pay my rent. I have worked for everything I have. I will keep working as much as the rotations allow. I just need a bridge until I qualify in January 2028.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

There is a resident at the care home, Frau M., who is ninety-three and does not remember my name. Every Saturday she asks me where I come from, and every Saturday I say "Ghana," and she smiles and says "so far." Last month she held my hand and said, "You have good hands for a doctor." She does not know I am studying medicine. I had never told her.

I grew up in Accra, in a house full of medicine: my father's pharmacy on the ground floor, my mother coming home from night shifts at the maternity ward with stories of births that went right and births that went wrong. When I was fourteen, a fire destroyed the pharmacy and most of our home. We lived with relatives for two years while my parents rebuilt everything from zero. I learned then that you can lose almost everything and still keep going.

I came to Switzerland at twenty-three, after working two years as a laboratory technician in Accra. I completed my bachelor of medicine at the University of Fribourg, while working the whole time, and now I am in the master's programme in Bern.

At the university, I was elected class representative for two years, and I co-founded a peer group for international medical students, who often feel very alone in the Swiss system. I also help organise health information evenings for the Ghanaian community in Bern.

I want to become a general practitioner, and later work in rural health in Ghana, where one doctor serves tens of thousands of people.

I have good hands, Frau M. says. I want to give them the chance to become a doctor's hands.
