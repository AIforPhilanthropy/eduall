In the interest of accuracy, I should say plainly that my household currently covers my costs.

I study Nursing part-time at BFH, which the school offers precisely for people who already work in care. I work about 24 hours a week as a care assistant in a home for people with dementia and earn CHF 2,702 a month. My spouse works in logistics at a pharmaceutical company in Bern, with a household income of around CHF 5,200, and contributes CHF 390 to my share of our expenses. I also receive a scholarship of CHF 4,321 a year from an Indonesian nursing foundation, and have savings of CHF 3,225. My own monthly costs are CHF 1,887.

My reason for applying is the structure of the coming years. From 2027/28 the programme includes clinical placements of several weeks each, which are full-time and unpaid, with rotating shifts. During those weeks I cannot work at the care home, and my scholarship ends in December. My spouse's income must also cover our car loan and support for my parents in Surabaya. My degree runs until July 2030, and a stable contribution in the placement year would allow me to keep my job without falling behind.

If the committee decides that others need the support more urgently, I will fully understand.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

On a cold evening in February 2025, in my first week at the Bern University of Applied Sciences (BFH), I stood outside the BFH health building on Murtenstrasse with a stack of German anatomy notes and realised that I understood almost none of it. I had been a nurse for nine years. I had worked through a pandemic. And here I was, starting again, unable to read my own textbook.

That was the moment I decided to treat my studies the way I had treated my toughest shifts: one task at a time. Two and a half years later I have completed 11 courses, and I am about to begin my final placement in intensive care.

I grew up in Surabaya. My father is an engineer and my mother a teacher. I trained as a nurse and worked in a public hospital, becoming head nurse of the emergency department at twenty-eight, leading a team of fifteen. During the COVID-19 pandemic our hospital was overwhelmed. We lost patients, and colleagues, in numbers I still cannot say out loud. I worked for six months without a real day off. Afterwards, I struggled with exhaustion and grief, and it took me a long time to recover.

When my spouse found work in Bern, I decided to use the move to become the nurse I wanted to be: one with a scientific foundation, able to lead change, not only survive it.

After graduating, I want to work in intensive care and later specialise in crisis preparedness for hospitals, so that no nursing team is left as unprepared as mine was.
