## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

How long can a person live on savings that were never meant to be savings? I ask myself this every month.

The CHF 6,608 in my account is what is left from the sale of my late grandmother's land in the Volta region, which my family agreed to use for my education. It is not a safety net. It is my whole plan. I spend CHF 2,121 a month, and my only regular support is CHF 220 from my older brother, a teacher in Ho whose household lives on about CHF 770. So every month about CHF 1,901 leaves my account and does not come back. In four months it will be empty.

I have never had a job. I want to be honest about that, and about why: for most of my twenties I was ill with spinal tuberculosis, first bedridden, then learning to walk again. By the time I was well, I was thirty-one, with no work history and a strong wish to become a nurse.

I also must be honest about my grades. My GPA is 2.4, and my standing is at risk. French is still hard for me, and the first year of nursing was a shock. I passed the practical modules well, but I failed two theory exams and must retake them.

I have not found a student job yet, although I am applying everywhere. If EduAll can help me, I will not waste it. I have waited too long for this chance.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

For three years I lay in a hospital bed in Ho, in eastern Ghana, with tuberculosis in my spine. I was twenty-two when it began. The doctors were busy and tired. It was the nurses who kept me alive, and not only with medicine. One nurse, Sister Abena, came every evening, even when she was not on duty, and read the newspaper to me. She told me I would walk again. I did not believe her. She was right.

My parents are teachers. They spent everything on my treatment. When I finally walked out of that hospital, slowly, with a cane, I knew what I would do with my life.

It took a long time. I had to rebuild my strength, then find a way to study. In 2025 I came to Lausanne to begin nursing. I am older than almost everyone, I speak French with difficulty, and I am slower than the others in exams. But on the wards, patients trust me. I think they can see that I know what it is to be in their bed.

I want to be Sister Abena for someone else. I want to become a nurse in rehabilitation, for people who have to learn to walk again.
