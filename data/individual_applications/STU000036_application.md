## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I will be completely open about my finances, because I think my application should be judged on accurate information.

My monthly expenses are CHF 1,955, including CHF 984 in rent for a room in a shared flat in Lausanne and CHF 245 in health insurance. I earn CHF 1,173 a month as a laboratory technician at a research institute in Épalinges, working the maximum hours my permit allows. My aunt in Kisumu, a primary school teacher whose household lives on about CHF 870 a month, insists on sending me CHF 240 every month. The resulting shortfall is only CHF 542 a month.

I also have savings of CHF 13,052. I accumulated them over seven years of work as a technician in Kenya, living rent-free with my aunt, and in my first year here, and I have protected them carefully. They are intended to carry me through the period between my master's and a doctoral position, which in my field often means several months without income while applying.

Why apply, then? Principally for my aunt. She has sent me money every month since I left Kenya, from a salary that barely covers her own household. I have asked her to stop; she refuses, as long as she thinks I need it. If I receive support from EduAll, I can tell her truthfully that I do not.

I recognise that many applicants will be in far greater need, and I would fully understand a decision to prioritise them.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

I am thirty-one years old, a biochemist in training, and a person who was raised by an aunt, a grandmother and a whole village.

My parents both died when I was twelve, within a year of each other, from illnesses that were treatable but not treated. I went to live with my aunt in Kisumu, who took in three children in addition to her own. She had trained as a teacher at a college, but in our family no one had ever been to a university. When I was admitted to Maseno University, she sold two of her goats to pay my first fees.

After my bachelor's degree I worked for seven years as a laboratory technician at a malaria research station near Kisumu. I ran assays, maintained mosquito colonies and trained younger technicians. I also co-founded a community science club that brought secondary school students into the laboratory on Saturdays, many of them girls who had never seen a microscope.

In 2025 I came to Lausanne for a Master's in Molecular Life Sciences, focusing on biochemistry. It has been an extraordinary experience. My GPA is 3.78, with an average of 98.0, and my supervisor has encouraged me to apply for doctoral positions. My thesis examines how the malaria parasite regulates its metabolism under drug pressure, a question I first encountered in Kisumu.

In Lausanne I help coordinate the East African community association's mentoring programme for new students.

My goal is to lead a research group in East Africa working on drug resistance in malaria. For me, education is the repayment of a debt to my aunt and to every person who decided that an orphan from Kisumu was worth investing in.
