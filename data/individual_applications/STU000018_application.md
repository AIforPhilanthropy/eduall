## Section 4 — Financial Need Statement

*Instructions: Describe your financial situation and explain why you need EduAll support to pursue your studies in Switzerland. Address why paying for your education is difficult, which costs are most challenging for you, what would happen to your studies without support, and whether you have exhausted other funding options (300–400 words).*

I have been studying Economics at the University of Zurich part-time for three years now, and I want to tell you honestly how my money works, because it has never been simple.

Each month I need CHF 2,135. My rent is CHF 861 for a small room in Schwamendingen, and my health insurance is CHF 490, which is a lot for me, but with my illness I cannot choose the cheapest model. I work about 21 hours a week: I clean offices in the early morning and I work two evenings in a care home. Together this gives me CHF 2,365. My older sister, who works as a care assistant in Paris and lives on about CHF 2,500 with her two children, still sends me CHF 300, even though I asked her to stop.

So on paper I cover my costs, and I want to say this clearly. I also have CHF 1,341 on my account. But this balance only works when I am healthy. When I am in hospital I lose my income from one day to the next; last winter it happened twice. And in May my mother in Thiès was told her kidneys are failing. She needs dialysis three times a week, and every franc I send to Senegal is a franc less for my studies.

I have six semesters left and I finish in July 2029. I am asking EduAll for a safety margin, so that one hospital stay or one more bill from Thiès does not force me to stop my studies.

## Section 5 — Personal Statement

*Instructions: Tell us who you are and where you come from, describe your educational goals and aspirations, share any personal challenges or circumstances you wish to (optional), and explain what education means to you (max. 500 words).*

There is a bench at the Zurich Stadelhofen station where I sat one evening in February 2023 and almost decided to give up. I had just come out of the hospital after a sickle-cell crisis. I had missed two exams. I did not understand half of what the professor said in German, and I was thirty-six years old among students who could be my children. I sat there for maybe an hour. Then I took the tram home, opened my notes and started again.

I was born in Thiès, in Senegal. My mother sold fabric at the market, my father repaired radios. I have lived with sickle-cell disease all my life. As a child I missed school for weeks every year. Teachers told my mother not to waste money on my education. She did not listen to them.

I came to Switzerland in 2011 for marriage. The marriage ended, but I stayed, and I worked. I cleaned hotel rooms, I worked in a laundry, I cared for old people. For ten years I learned German in the evenings. When I obtained my C permit, I decided it was finally my turn to study.

Studying economics was a promise I made to the women of my mother's market. They borrowed money at crazy interest rates, and nobody explained anything to them. I want to understand how credit can help poor women instead of trapping them.

My grades are not brilliant. My GPA is 2.99. Every semester I lose weeks to my illness. But I have completed 22 courses, one by one, and I am still here.

After my degree I want to work for a microfinance institution in West Africa, or for an organisation in Switzerland that supports women's economic projects. I want to show the teachers of my childhood that my mother was right.
